# Professional Mode

Version 2.1.0 · Human Signal

## Intended use

Workplace writing that is neither purely technical nor personal: business memos, proposals, status updates, meeting summaries, performance feedback, policy documents, client correspondence, and internal announcements.

Use this mode when the reader needs to make a decision, understand a situation, or act, and when the relationship is professional rather than intimate or academic.

## Tone

Clear, restrained, and respectful. Confident without overselling; direct without being blunt. The writer takes responsibility for decisions and does not hide behind passive constructions.

Professional tone is not the same as formal tone. A memo can be plain and still be professional.

## Vocabulary

- Plain, specific business vocabulary: "decision," "cost," "deadline," "owner," "risk."
- Precision about quantities: budget, headcount, dates, percentages, and their sources.
- Ownership language: "The platform team will…" rather than "It is expected that…"
- Avoid: "synergy," "leverage," "align," "optics," "bandwidth" (as attention), "circle back," "move the needle," "best-in-class," "world-class."
- Avoid euphemism for hard facts: "reduction in force" when the reader needs to know how many roles.
- Use "we" for collective decisions the writer shares; use "I" for commitments the writer owns.

## Sentence characteristics

- Medium length, declarative, one main point per sentence.
- Active voice with named owners.
- Conditions stated before consequences.
- Numbers with units and time frames.
- Avoid nominalization: "decided" rather than "made a decision," "failed" rather than "experienced a failure."
- Avoid stacked politeness in requests; one marker is enough.

## Paragraph structure

- Lead with the decision, the request, or the bottom line.
- Follow with the reason, then the cost or risk, then the next step.
- One issue per paragraph.
- Use a short bullet list for actions with owners and dates.
- Keep the memo to the length the decision requires; longer is not more thorough.

## Common mistakes

1. **Burying the decision.** Context first, conclusion last, reader impatient.
2. **Passive responsibility.** "It was decided" with no owner.
3. **Euphemism.** Softening layoffs, delays, or failures until the reader cannot tell what happened.
4. **False consensus.** "We all agree" when there was disagreement.
5. **Unquantified impact.** "Significant cost savings" with no figure.
6. **Recommendation without a cost.** Benefits listed, trade-offs omitted.
7. **Ceremonial padding.** "Please be advised," "kindly note," "at your earliest convenience."
8. **Forced positivity.** Enthusiasm that undercuts the seriousness of a problem.
9. **Action items without owners or dates.**
10. **Excessive hedging.** "Potentially may consider" in a document that must commit.

## Editing rules

1. Put the bottom line in the first two sentences.
2. Name the owner of every action.
3. Quantify impact where the data exists; state the source.
4. State the recommendation and the main trade-off together.
5. Replace euphemism with the fact, stated plainly and without cruelty.
6. Remove ceremony that does not change the relationship.
7. Preserve the writer's authority; do not add hedges they did not use.
8. Keep disagreement visible when it is material to the decision.
9. Do not add motivational language to a factual document.
10. End with the next step, not a summary.

## Examples

**Before:**
> In today's fast-paced business environment, it is essential that we leverage cross-functional synergies to drive meaningful outcomes. To that end, a decision has been made to explore potential process optimizations.

**After:**
> We are merging the two approval queues into one. This should cut the median approval time from five days to two. The trade-off is that the finance team loses its separate escalation path; we will review that after four weeks.

**Why:** It states the decision, the expected effect, the cost, and the review point.

**Before:**
> Unfortunately, due to unforeseen circumstances, we will be implementing a workforce optimization initiative.

**After:**
> We are eliminating 40 roles, about 8% of the company, concentrated in the sales operations team. Affected employees will be notified today and will receive 12 weeks of severance.

**Why:** It states the scale, the location, the timing, and the support without euphemism.

**Before:**
> Please be advised that the project timeline has been adjusted and stakeholders will be aligned accordingly.

**After:**
> The launch moves from 12 May to 2 June. The delay comes from the payment provider's certification, which took three weeks longer than planned. Marketing will keep the original campaign dates.

**Why:** It gives the new date, the cause, and the consequence for another team.

**Before:**
> It is recommended that further consideration be given to the proposal.

**After:**
> Approve the proposal. It costs $18,000 and saves about 300 support hours a year.

**Why:** It makes a recommendation with a cost and a benefit.

## Checklist

- [ ] Bottom line is in the first two sentences.
- [ ] Every action has an owner and a date.
- [ ] Impact is quantified or explicitly estimated.
- [ ] Trade-offs are stated.
- [ ] Responsibility is attributed.
- [ ] No euphemism for material facts.
- [ ] No ceremonial padding.
- [ ] No forced positivity.
- [ ] Disagreement is visible if material.
- [ ] The ending states the next step.
