# Academic Mode

Version 2.1.0 · Human Signal

## Intended use

Formal analysis, argument, and research writing: essays, research papers, literature reviews, theses, conference submissions, grant narratives, and critical analysis. Use this mode when the reader expects evidence, attribution, and a defensible line of reasoning.

Do not use it for documentation, internal email, or marketing copy. Academic register in those contexts reads as inflated and distant.

## Tone

Analytical, measured, and explicit. The writer's stance is visible through the argument, not through enthusiasm or self-reference. Confidence is calibrated: assertions match evidence, and uncertainty is stated where it exists.

Avoid both extremes: the breathless ("This groundbreaking study revolutionizes…") and the evacuated ("It could perhaps be tentatively suggested that…"). The target is a writer who has thought carefully and is willing to be held to their claims.

## Vocabulary

- Use the field's standard terminology precisely. Do not paraphrase a term of art into plain language if the term names a specific construct.
- Define terms that are contested, newly introduced, or used in a nonstandard sense.
- Prefer specific verbs to evaluative adjectives: "reduced," "predicted," "correlated" rather than "significant," "impactful," "robust."
- Use hedges with referents: "in this sample," "for the two sites studied," not "arguably" or "perhaps."
- Avoid: "delve," "landscape," "realm," "tapestry," "multifaceted," "paradigm shift," "plays a crucial role," "it is well known that."
- Keep spelling and citation style consistent with the venue (APA, MLA, Chicago, IEEE, Vancouver, or house style).

## Sentence characteristics

- Mix simple and complex sentences according to the complexity of the relation being expressed. A causal claim with two conditions may need a complex sentence; a finding needs a short one.
- Keep the main claim in the main clause. Do not bury the finding in a subordinate clause after "although."
- Use passive voice where the actor is irrelevant or unknown ("samples were stored at −80 °C"), active where responsibility matters ("two coders independently labeled…").
- Keep subjects and verbs close. Academic prose often fails here, with long noun phrases between them.
- Avoid nominalization chains: "the operationalization of the conceptualization of…"
- Use parallelism for lists of findings or criteria.

## Paragraph structure

- One claim per paragraph, usually stated early, followed by evidence and analysis.
- Evidence should be adjacent to the claim it supports, with the source and its limits clear.
- Address counterarguments in their strongest form.
- Paragraph length follows the weight of the claim. A central finding may need three paragraphs; a caveat may need one sentence.
- Transitions should encode real logical relations, not announce that a new section has begun.

## Common mistakes

1. **Importance inflation.** Opening with a claim that the topic is crucial, without showing for whom or why.
2. **Literature-list paragraphs.** Summarizing sources one by one instead of synthesizing by claim.
3. **Citation decoration.** Attaching a source to a sentence it does not support.
4. **Causal overreach.** "Caused" for a correlational design; "proves" for a single study.
5. **Unnamed authority.** "Researchers agree" with no citation.
6. **Summary conclusion.** Repeating the introduction instead of stating what follows.
7. **Over-hedging.** Stacking "may," "possibly," and "suggests" until the claim disappears.
8. **Register mismatch.** Chatty asides or contractions in a formal journal submission.
9. **Undefined terms.** Using a key construct without saying how it is operationalized.
10. **Fabricated specificity.** A precise statistic with no source.

## Editing rules

1. Verify every citation before keeping it. Remove or flag anything unverifiable.
2. Match the verb to the design: causes, predicts, is associated with, suggests.
3. State the scope of every generalization.
4. Keep qualifications; they are part of the claim.
5. Separate results from interpretation.
6. Replace "very important" with the consequence that makes it important.
7. Remove sentences that only announce structure.
8. Check that the conclusion adds no new claim.
9. Preserve technical terminology even when it repeats.
10. Keep the author's theoretical position; clarify it, do not redirect it.

## Examples

**Before:**
> In today's rapidly changing academic landscape, it is more important than ever to understand the multifaceted nature of student engagement. This topic plays a crucial role in educational outcomes and has been studied by many researchers.

**After:**
> Student engagement predicts course completion, but the construct is measured at least three different ways in the literature: time on task, self-report scales, and observed participation. These measures correlate weakly with one another, which complicates comparisons across studies.

**Why:** The revision replaces framing with a specific problem (measurement divergence) and a consequence for the field.

**Before:**
> The results were very significant and proved that the intervention works.

**After:**
> The intervention group scored 6.2 points higher on the post-test (95% CI 2.1–10.3). The design cannot rule out selection effects, because students were not randomly assigned.

**Why:** The claim is stated with its uncertainty and its design limitation.

**Before:**
> Furthermore, it is important to note that the limitations of this study should be considered.

**After:**
> The sample was drawn from one university, so the estimates may not transfer to institutions with different admission criteria.

**Why:** The limitation is specific and tied to the claim it limits.

## Checklist

- [ ] Thesis or research question is explicit.
- [ ] Every empirical claim is attributable.
- [ ] Causal language matches the design.
- [ ] Terms are defined or standard.
- [ ] Evidence is adjacent to claim.
- [ ] Counterarguments are addressed fairly.
- [ ] Limitations are specific.
- [ ] Conclusion follows from the evidence.
- [ ] Register matches the venue.
- [ ] Citations are verified and consistent.
