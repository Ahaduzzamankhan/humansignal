# Creative Mode

Version 2.1.0 · Human Signal

## Intended use

Fiction, personal essays, narrative nonfiction, poetry, scripts, and expressive writing where style is part of the content. Use this mode when the reader has chosen to spend time with the writing as writing, not only to extract information.

Do not apply this mode to documentation, contracts, or reports. In those genres, expressive variation costs precision.

## Tone

Expressive, deliberate, and appropriate to the piece. Tone may be wry, somber, intimate, distant, lyrical, or plain. What matters is that the tone is consistent and that it serves the subject.

Creative tone is not "flowery." A spare sentence can be more expressive than an ornate one.

## Vocabulary

- Concrete nouns and precise verbs carry the image: "the gate stuck in the frost," not "the entrance was problematic."
- Figurative language should map onto something real. A metaphor earns its place by revealing a property, not by decorating a noun.
- Repetition can be a device: a refrain, a motif, a rhythm. Keep it if it is controlled.
- Dialect and idiolect are valid when consistent and legible; do not sand them into standard English.
- Avoid default poetic vocabulary ("whisper," "shimmer," "soul," "eternity," "tapestry") unless the piece has earned it.

## Sentence characteristics

- Sentence length is a tool. A long sentence can accumulate; a short one can land.
- Fragments, run-ons, and unconventional punctuation are permitted when they serve voice and remain readable.
- Rhythm should be audible. Read the passage aloud; if you run out of breath in the wrong place, the sentence is misbuilt.
- Repetition of structure can create incantation or monotony; the difference is control.
- Do not vary for the sake of variety. A paragraph of short sentences may be exactly right.

## Paragraph structure

- Paragraphs can be one line or one page; the break is a beat.
- White space carries meaning: isolation, pause, acceleration.
- Dialogue follows its own conventions; keep attribution minimal when the speakers are distinct.
- Scene and summary alternate; do not summarize what should be dramatized, or dramatize what should be compressed.
- Structure may be nonlinear, but the reader must be able to orient.

## Common mistakes

1. **Ornament without image.** Adjectives and metaphors that do not let the reader see anything.
2. **Uniform lyricism.** Every sentence striving for beauty, so none lands.
3. **Abstract emotion.** "A deep sense of melancholy washed over her" instead of a concrete detail that produces it.
4. **Mechanical variation.** Alternating long and short sentences because a guide said to.
5. **Cliché.** "Heart pounded," "breath caught," "a single tear" used without fresh context.
6. **Over-explanation.** Telling the reader what a scene means instead of letting it mean.
7. **Inconsistent voice.** Narration that shifts register without a reason.
8. **Forced symbolism.** Objects that exist only to carry a theme.
9. **Dialogue that explains.** Characters saying what they both already know for the reader's benefit.
10. **Purple prose in a plain scene.** Style that overwhelms the subject.

## Editing rules

1. Preserve the writer's voice above all. Creative editing is the most invasive kind; make the smallest change that helps.
2. Cut abstraction before cutting sentences. Replace a vague emotion with the detail that causes it.
3. Read aloud. Fix what trips the tongue.
4. Remove metaphors that do not map; keep the ones that reveal.
5. Do not add sensory detail the writer did not choose. You may point out where it is missing.
6. Keep deliberate fragments and unusual punctuation.
7. Do not resolve ambiguity that is doing work.
8. Check consistency of voice, tense, and point of view.
9. Prefer the concrete verb to the adverbial phrase.
10. Do not impose a moral or a summary the piece does not earn.

## Examples

**Before:**
> A profound sense of melancholy washed over her as she contemplated the myriad possibilities of her existence.

**After:**
> She stood at the kitchen window with the second cup going cold, watching the neighbor's dog test the fence.

**Why:** The feeling is displaced onto concrete detail; the reader infers the state without being told.

**Before:**
> The ancient, mysterious forest was a tapestry of wonders, and every step revealed new and incredible secrets.

**After:**
> The path narrowed where the birches leaned in. Twice she heard water, and twice it was only the wind in the leaves.

**Why:** It replaces evaluative description with sensory specifics and a small uncertainty.

**Before:**
> "I can't believe you did this," she said, expressing her deep anger and betrayal at his actions.

**After:**
> "You told them." She set the glass down without looking at him.

**Why:** Dialogue and gesture carry the accusation and the anger.

**Before:**
> He was sad. He walked home. The rain fell. He thought about his life. It was hard. He kept walking.

**After:**
> He walked home in the rain, past the lit windows of the houses he had almost bought, and did not hurry.

**Why:** The short sentences were not creating rhythm; they were listing facts. One sentence with a specific detail does more.

## Checklist

- [ ] The voice is the writer's, not the editor's.
- [ ] Figurative language maps to something real.
- [ ] Concrete detail carries emotion where possible.
- [ ] Sentence length serves the scene.
- [ ] Repetition is controlled.
- [ ] Dialogue sounds like the characters.
- [ ] Tense and point of view are consistent.
- [ ] No forced symbolism or moral.
- [ ] Read aloud without stumbling.
- [ ] The piece does not explain its own effect.
