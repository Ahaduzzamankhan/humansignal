# Academic Writing

Version 2.1.0 · Human Signal

Academic writing is an evidence practice before it is a style. This file covers the genres Human Signal supports, the conventions that make them readable, and the integrity rules that override every stylistic preference.

Related: [context-awareness.md](context-awareness.md) · [../modes/academic.md](../modes/academic.md) · [../examples/academic-examples.md](../examples/academic-examples.md) · [editing-guidelines.md](editing-guidelines.md)

## Non-negotiable integrity rules

1. **Never fabricate a source.** No invented authors, titles, journals, years, DOIs, URLs, page numbers, or dataset names.
2. **Never invent findings.** Do not attribute a result to a study unless the result is verifiable.
3. **Never claim to have read a source you have not read.** If you have only seen an abstract, say so where it matters.
4. **Distinguish evidence from interpretation.** A measured effect is not the same as the explanation for it.
5. **Preserve qualifications.** "Associated with" is not "causes." "In this sample" is not "in general."
6. **Do not pad citations.** A reference should support the specific claim it is attached to.
7. **Report methods honestly.** Include the details a competent reader needs to judge or reproduce the work.
8. **Disclose limitations that affect the conclusion.** Not every limitation, but the ones that bear on the claim.

If a claim cannot be verified, the correct move is to state the uncertainty or remove the claim — not to smooth it over.

## 1. Essays

**Purpose.** Develop and defend a thesis through reasoning, evidence, and interpretation.

**Structure.** Introduction (question, thesis, roadmap if needed) → body paragraphs (one claim each, with evidence and analysis) → conclusion (what follows from the argument, not a recap).

**What makes it academic.**
- A thesis that is arguable and specific.
- Claims supported by reasoning or cited evidence.
- Counterarguments addressed rather than ignored.
- Interpretation separated from description.

**Common weaknesses.**
- A thesis that describes rather than argues ("This essay will discuss X").
- Paragraphs organized by source ("Smith says… Jones says…") rather than by claim.
- Quotation without analysis.
- A conclusion that repeats the introduction.

**Editing guidance.**
- Make the thesis a claim someone could dispute.
- Put the claim first in each body paragraph; let evidence follow.
- After every quotation, add a sentence explaining what it shows.
- Replace "many scholars argue" with named positions and specific disagreements.

**Example.**
- Weak: "Social media has many effects on teenagers. This essay will explore them."
- Better: "Time spent on social media predicts sleep disruption more strongly than it predicts mood change in the studies reviewed here, which suggests sleep is the more plausible pathway."

## 2. Research papers

**Purpose.** Report original work so that others can evaluate and build on it.

**Structure (IMRaD).** Introduction (problem, gap, question, contribution) → Methods (design, materials, procedure, analysis) → Results (findings, without interpretation) → Discussion (interpretation, limitations, implications) → Conclusion.

**What makes it credible.**
- A clearly stated research question.
- Methods detailed enough to assess validity.
- Results reported with uncertainty (confidence intervals, standard errors, sample sizes).
- Interpretation tied to the results, not to expectations.
- Limitations that name the threat to validity.

**Common weaknesses.**
- Introduction that reviews everything except the gap being addressed.
- Methods missing randomization, blinding, inclusion criteria, or software versions.
- Results that include interpretation ("surprisingly, X caused Y").
- Discussion that overstates generalizability.
- Conclusion that claims more than the design supports.

**Editing guidance.**
- Keep the gap statement to one or two sentences.
- Move interpretation out of Results.
- Pair every causal verb with the design that licenses it.
- Replace "proves" with "supports," "is consistent with," or "suggests," as appropriate.

## 3. Literature reviews

**Purpose.** Synthesize what is known, identify disagreements, and locate a gap.

**Two valid organizations.**
- **Chronological:** useful when the field's questions changed over time.
- **Thematic:** usually stronger; organize by question, mechanism, or method rather than by author.

**What makes it a review rather than a list.**
- Explicit inclusion criteria (databases, dates, keywords, languages).
- Comparison across studies on the same dimensions.
- Attention to conflicting findings and why they conflict.
- An assessment of evidence quality, not just a count of studies.
- A gap that follows from the synthesis.

**Common weaknesses.**
- Study-by-study summary with no synthesis.
- Treating all studies as equally reliable.
- Ignoring null results.
- Citing a review instead of the primary studies for a specific finding.
- A gap that is merely "no one has studied this exact combination."

**Editing guidance.**
- Build a comparison table (study, sample, method, finding, limitation) before drafting.
- Use topic sentences that state a pattern ("Three of the four trials found…").
- Name the reason for disagreement: sample, measure, dose, duration, or analysis.
- Distinguish "no evidence of effect" from "evidence of no effect."

## 4. Scientific explanations

**Purpose.** Explain a mechanism or phenomenon accurately to a defined audience.

**Structure.** Phenomenon → mechanism → evidence → boundary conditions → significance.

**What good looks like.**
- The level of detail matches the audience's background.
- Mechanisms are described in causal order.
- Quantities include units and uncertainty.
- Analogies are marked and bounded.
- Competing explanations are acknowledged where they exist.

**Common weaknesses.**
- Teleological language ("the cell wants to…") where a mechanism is meant.
- Mechanism asserted without evidence.
- Scale confusion (mixing molecular, cellular, and organismal levels).
- Overconfident extrapolation from model organisms.

**Editing guidance.**
- Replace purpose language with mechanism language unless purpose is genuinely at issue.
- State the level of analysis explicitly when the text moves between levels.
- Add the condition under which the mechanism fails or reverses.

## 5. Arguments

**Purpose.** Establish a conclusion through reasons that the audience can evaluate.

**Components.** Claim, reasons, evidence, warrants (the assumptions linking reasons to claim), qualifications, rebuttals.

**What makes an argument academic.**
- The conclusion is stated.
- Each reason is distinguishable and supported.
- The warrant is made explicit when it is contestable.
- Objections are stated at their strongest.
- The conclusion's scope matches the evidence.

**Common weaknesses.**
- Conclusion asserted, not argued.
- Reasons that restate the conclusion.
- Unstated assumptions doing the real work.
- Strawman objections.
- False balance between well-supported and fringe positions.

**Editing guidance.**
- Write the argument as a numbered chain before drafting prose.
- For each link, ask "what would a skeptic say here?"
- State the strongest objection in its own terms, then answer it.
- Qualify the conclusion to the population and conditions studied.

## 6. Comparative analysis

**Purpose.** Explain similarities and differences between two or more cases, texts, systems, or periods, and say why they matter.

**Structure.** Criteria for comparison → case-by-case or criterion-by-criterion treatment → analysis of differences → significance.

**Criterion-by-criterion is usually stronger** because it keeps the comparison visible. Case-by-case works when each case needs narrative coherence.

**Common weaknesses.**
- Comparing on unstated or inconsistent criteria.
- Treating differences as significant without explaining why.
- Parallel structure that forces false symmetry.
- Description without analysis.

**Editing guidance.**
- State the comparison criteria before comparing.
- Use a table for parallel attributes, prose for interpretation.
- For each difference, state the consequence.
- Allow asymmetry: one case may be more developed if that is what the evidence supports.

## 7. Reports

**Purpose.** Inform a decision or document a process.

**Structure.** Summary → context → method → findings → analysis → recommendations → appendices.

**What good looks like.**
- The summary states the decision or finding, not the topic.
- Findings are separated from recommendations.
- Recommendations name an owner, a cost, and a condition.
- Data sources and dates are stated.
- Assumptions are visible.

**Common weaknesses.**
- Executive summary that describes the report instead of its conclusion.
- Recommendations with no implementation path.
- Unlabeled estimates presented as measurements.
- Missing denominators.

**Editing guidance.**
- Lead with the decision the reader must make.
- Put numbers in a table with units and sources.
- Separate "what we found" from "what we recommend."
- State what would change the recommendation.

## 8. Critical analysis

**Purpose.** Evaluate an argument, text, artifact, or practice against explicit criteria.

**Structure.** Object of analysis → criteria → evidence → evaluation → implications.

**What good looks like.**
- Criteria are stated and justified.
- The object is represented fairly before it is criticized.
- Evaluation is supported by specific evidence from the object.
- Alternative interpretations are considered.
- The judgment is proportionate.

**Common weaknesses.**
- Opinion presented as analysis.
- Criteria applied inconsistently.
- Critique of a position the author does not hold.
- Summary substituting for evaluation.

**Editing guidance.**
- State the criteria in advance.
- Quote or cite the specific passage being evaluated.
- Distinguish "the argument fails" from "I disagree."
- Acknowledge the strongest part of the object before the critique.

## 9. Evidence-based claims

**Rule.** The strength of the verb must match the strength of the design.

| Design | Licensed language |
|---|---|
| Randomized controlled trial | "reduced," "caused" (with conditions), "led to" |
| Quasi-experiment | "was associated with," "predicts" (with caveats) |
| Observational study | "is associated with," "correlates with" |
| Case study | "in this case," "illustrates," "suggests" |
| Model or simulation | "the model predicts," "under assumptions X and Y" |
| Expert opinion | "argues," "contends," "recommends" |

**Prohibited moves.**
- Causal language for correlational data.
- "Significant" for a result that was not tested for statistical significance (or vice versa: statistical significance is not practical importance).
- Generalizing from a convenience sample to a population.
- Citing a preprint as peer-reviewed.
- Treating a single study as settled knowledge.

**Editing guidance.** Read each claim and ask what design would license it. If the text cites a weaker design, change the verb.

## 10. Citations

**Purpose.** Attribute ideas, allow verification, and locate the work within a literature.

**Principles.**
- Cite the source of the specific claim, not a general nod to the field.
- Cite primary sources for specific findings; reviews for synthesis.
- Match the required style exactly (APA, MLA, Chicago, IEEE, Vancouver, or the venue's own).
- Include page numbers for direct quotations and specific paraphrases where the style requires them.
- Keep the reference list consistent: every in-text citation appears, and every listed reference is cited.
- Do not cite a source you have not checked.

**Common errors.**
- Citation attached to the wrong clause.
- Multiple claims under one citation, only one of which the source supports.
- Secondary citation presented as primary ("as cited in" omitted).
- DOIs or URLs that do not resolve.
- Inconsistent author-name formats.

**Editing guidance.** Build the reference list first, then attach citations to claims one by one. Verify each entry against the source record.

## 11. Research summaries

**Purpose.** Compress a study or body of work without distorting it.

**What to include.** Question, design, sample, key result with effect size and uncertainty, main limitation, and relevance.

**What to leave out.** Methodological minutiae that do not affect interpretation, and rhetorical framing that inflates importance.

**Common weaknesses.**
- Reporting a result without the sample or design.
- Omitting the limitation that changes the interpretation.
- Using the abstract's language without checking the results section.
- Presenting a subgroup finding as the main finding.

**Editing guidance.** Follow the order: what was asked, how it was studied, what was found, what it means, what it cannot tell us.

## 12. Methodology sections

**Purpose.** Allow a competent reader to evaluate and, where possible, reproduce the work.

**Include.**
- Design and rationale.
- Participants or materials, with inclusion and exclusion criteria.
- Procedure, in chronological order.
- Measures and instruments, with versions.
- Analysis plan, including software and settings.
- Deviations from the plan, with reasons.
- Ethics approval and consent, where applicable.

**Write in past tense** for what was done. Use passive voice when the actor is irrelevant ("samples were stored"), active when responsibility matters ("two coders independently labeled…").

**Common weaknesses.**
- Missing sample size justification.
- Vague measures ("we measured engagement").
- Analysis described after results, making it look post hoc.
- Software versions omitted.
- No statement about missing data.

---

## 13. Discussion sections

**Purpose.** Interpret the results, relate them to prior work, state limitations, and draw bounded implications.

**Structure.** Principal finding → comparison with prior work → explanation → limitations → implications.

**What good looks like.**
- The first paragraph states what was found, not what was studied.
- Explanations are offered as explanations, with alternatives.
- Limitations are specific and tied to the claim.
- Implications are proportionate.

**Common weaknesses.**
- Repeating the Results section.
- Over-interpreting a small or noisy effect.
- Ignoring disconfirming evidence.
- Limitations listed but not connected to conclusions.
- Implications for practice that outrun the data.

**Editing guidance.** For each implication, ask: which result supports it, and what would make it wrong? If the answer is not in the paper, cut the implication.

---

## 14. Conclusions

**Purpose.** State what the work establishes and what remains open.

**What to include.**
- The central finding in one or two sentences.
- The condition under which it holds.
- The contribution to the field.
- The next question, if it follows from the work.

**What to avoid.**
- New evidence.
- New claims not supported earlier.
- A summary of every section.
- Grand statements about the field's future.
- "Further research is needed" without a specific question.

**Editing guidance.** A conclusion should be shorter than the discussion and should not introduce a concept the reader has not seen.

## Academic style: what to keep and what to cut

**Keep.**
- Precision in terminology.
- Explicit reasoning.
- Hedging where the evidence is uncertain.
- Formal register where the venue expects it.
- Passive voice where the actor is irrelevant.
- Citations and qualifications.

**Cut.**
- Empty importance claims ("X has become increasingly important").
- Field-wide generalizations without citations.
- "It is well known that…" without a source.
- Decorative transitions.
- Summaries that repeat the introduction.
- Claims that outrun the evidence.
- Formulaic openings about the modern world.

**Do not "simplify" away precision.** A technical term that names a specific construct is not jargon to be replaced. "Working memory capacity" is not improved by "thinking space."

## Pre-submission checklist

- [ ] Research question is explicit and answerable.
- [ ] Thesis or contribution is stated.
- [ ] Every empirical claim is attributable.
- [ ] Every citation resolves and supports its sentence.
- [ ] Causal language matches the design.
- [ ] Sample, measures, and analysis are described.
- [ ] Results are separated from interpretation.
- [ ] Limitations are tied to the conclusions.
- [ ] The conclusion adds no new claims.
- [ ] Terminology is consistent across sections.
- [ ] Quotations are exact and page-referenced.
- [ ] Numbers, units, and dates are consistent.
- [ ] The register matches the venue.
- [ ] No fabricated or unverifiable references.
